This section brings together material from university lectures and useful online resources on designing, analysing, and applying algorithms. It starts with the foundations and gradually moves to more involved problems, so it is easy to revisit and easy for others to follow.

Where a page is based on a particular lecture, article, book, or video, its source should be linked at the end of that page. This makes it easy to find the original explanation and gives credit to its author.

## What this section helps with

The goal is to build a practical understanding of how to:

- describe an algorithm clearly and estimate how its running time grows;
- choose an appropriate approach for a problem, such as divide and conquer, dynamic programming, or a greedy method;
- represent graphs and use standard algorithms to explore them; and
- recognise common optimisation problems, including shortest paths, flows, and matchings.

## Topics

### 1. Foundations

1. **Introduction to algorithms** — asymptotic notation and complexity analysis.
2. **Sorting and selection** — heapsort, quicksort, linear-time sorting, medians, and order statistics.

### 2. Algorithm design techniques

3. **Dynamic programming** — solving a problem by combining solutions to smaller subproblems.
4. **Greedy algorithms** — making a locally best choice at each step.

### 3. Graph algorithms

5. **Graph fundamentals** — graph representations, breadth-first search (BFS), and depth-first search (DFS).
6. **Eulerian circuits** — finding a route that uses every edge exactly once in an undirected graph.
7. **Minimum spanning trees** — Kruskal's and Prim's algorithms.
8. **Shortest paths** — the Bellman–Ford algorithm.
9. **Shortest paths in directed acyclic graphs (DAGs)** — followed by Dijkstra's algorithm.

### 4. Analysis, flows, and matching

10. **Amortized analysis** — understanding the average cost of a sequence of operations.
11. **Network flow** — the Ford–Fulkerson method.
12. **Bipartite graphs and matching** — their connection to combinatorial optimisation.

## How to use this section

For each topic, begin with the problem an algorithm solves. Then work through a small example by hand, look at why it works, and compare its time and space requirements with other approaches. Following the topics in order provides a gentle path through the material.
